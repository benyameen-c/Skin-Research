## Damage scan: what is damaged in IGH white spots

<p><span class="tag lead">Leads only</span> Not in the evidence ledger. Not medical advice. Gate A has not been passed for any source here.</p>

### How to read this page

Every claim below carries a verbatim quote and a PMID. The quotes come from abstracts or open-access full texts saved in the project's excerpts folder (`pmid_<id>.txt`, `pmc_<id>.txt`). Most sources here were read as abstracts only, so their numbers, methods and caveats are `not in abstract` until a full text is read. Sentences marked **Inference** are this page's reasoning, not a finding from any paper.

Most IGH studies are small (often 3 to 20 people) and descriptive. None tested a cause by changing it and watching the spot.

### What the spots look like under the microscope

The most repeated finding is less melanin and fewer pigment cells (melanocytes) in the spot than in the skin around it, with a thin, flattened top layer.

- Joshi 2014, 55 biopsies (PMID 25484386): "The epidermis had markedly decreased to absent melanin in the basal layer and reduced numbers of melanocytes at the dermoepidermal junction." It also reports "small foci of retained melanin in the basal layer (skip areas) alternating with larger areas of melanin loss were present in almost 80% of cases."
- Kim 2010, 47 patients, lesion compared with skin next to it (PMID 20465639): "The IGH lesions demonstrated decreased melanin pigment and reduced numbers of melanocytes by NKI/beteb and MART-1." The flattened skin was less common than older reports said: "epidermal atrophy was present in only five cases (10.6%)".
- Falabella 1987 (PMID 3805391): "Epithelial atrophy, patchy absence of melanocytes and melanin, flattening of the rete pegs, and basket weave hyperkeratosis were the most prominent histologic findings".
- Reddy 2024, 100 patients (PMID 39324349): "reduced melanin globules or melanocytes, skip lesions, and flattening of rete ridges across all dermoscopic patterns."

### What happens to the pigment cells

The sources agree that melanocytes are fewer. They disagree on whether the ones left are healthy.

- Fewer cells, not stuck immature cells. Wallace 1998, nine lesions (PMID 9765023): "the pathogenesis of IGH involves an absolute decrease in the number of melanocytes; a block in melanocyte differentiation does not appear to be a major component of the process."
- Two stages. Ortonne 1980, three patients (PMID 7377803): "Depigmentation in IGH seems to occur in two stages, loss of melanogenic activity in certain malanocytes and elimination of inactive melanocytes." (The misspelling is in the abstract.)
- Not all gone. Wilson 1982, 20 cases (PMID 6183861): "In no case were melanocytes completely absent, excluding vitiligo."
- Fewer and smaller pigment granules. Savall 1980 (PMID 6779850): "a decreased number of melanosomes in the melanocytes with predominance of Stages I and II and small sizes." Kim 2010 (PMID 20465639): "The ultrastructural evaluation showed degenerative melanocytes and decreased melanosomes."
- Structurally normal cells. Kakepis 2015, four patients (PMID 25088925): "No significant structural abnormality of the melanocytes was observed".
- Counts. Gilhar 1989 (PMID 2508572) gives dopa-positive melanocytes in the spot versus the surrounding skin: "55.25 +/- 18.00 mm2 vs 220 +/- 28.28 mm2". Ploysangam 1990 (PMID 2229495): "significantly fewer dopa-positive melanocytes are in the white macules of idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis than in normal skin."
- A newer letter titled "Melanocytes in idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis disappear and are senescent" (Kim 2023, PMID 36394380) has no abstract; its content is `full text needed` (on the paywall list).

### Pigment transfer to the surrounding skin cells

Melanocytes make pigment and hand it to the surrounding skin cells (keratinocytes), which carry the colour. Three sources point to a fault in that hand-over, as well as in cell numbers.

- Kakepis 2015 (PMID 25088925): "In some areas, there was a reduced uptake of melanosomes by the keratinocytes." Its conclusion: "a functional defect in the transfer of melanosomes from the melanocytes to the keratinocytes."
- Rani 2018, six patients and six controls (PMID 29516488): "Melanocytes from IGH patients were bigger in size with very small and retracted dendrites as compared to the control melanocytes." The authors suggest pigment builds up inside the cell: "Accumulation of more melanin in the IGH melanocytes might be due to problem in the transfer of melanin from melanocytes to keratinocytes." That sentence is the authors' hypothesis, and these cells were grown in a dish.
- Arbache 2022, 20 patients, lesion compared with skin next to it (PMID 35114009): "unreported keratinocyte cytoplasmic changes on electron microscopy." Details are `full text needed`.
- Senescent melanocytes in a dish show the same pattern. Park 2023, open access, full text read (PMID 37554281): "in vitro senescent melanocytes are characterized by melanosome transport dysfunction resulting in melanin accumulation". The in-body check rests on one person: "We confirmed these findings in vivo in skin biopsies from a donor with IGH."

### Senescence, oxidative stress and the dermis

Senescent cells are cells that have permanently stopped dividing but stay in the tissue and release signals. Four IGH sources point this way, all small.

- Rani 2018 (PMID 29516488): "expression of p16, p21, and hp1 was significantly higher in lesional skin of IGH patient as compared to healthy controls." The comparison is with other people, not with the same person's normal skin.
- Park 2023 (PMID 37554281) cites an earlier finding, a secondary claim until the cited paper is read: "The number of p16Ink4a-expressing melanocytes was increased in hypopigmented IGH skin compared to perilesional normal skin".
- Kim 2026 (PMID 41453137), working in UV-damaged melanocytes in a dish: "ATG7 downregulation emerged as the earliest molecular alteration and was consistently found in senescent melanocytes and idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis skin." ATG7 is a gene needed for the cell's recycling system (autophagy). The same abstract reports that "Metformin treatment restored autophagic activity" in cultured cells; no IGH patient was treated. Human sample counts are `not in abstract`.
- Senescent fibroblasts (the dermis cells under the spot): Kim 2020, a letter titled "Role of senescent fibroblasts in the development of idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis" (PMID 31823346). No abstract; content is `full text needed`.
- Dermal scarring under the spot. Arbache 2022 (PMID 35114009): "We found superficial dermal fibrosis in 90% (18/20) of our IGH cases". The authors conclude: "Our results suggest an interaction between melanocytes and the subjacent dermis in IGH."
- Outside IGH, aged fibroblasts change pigment in lab-grown skin. Duval 2014 (PMID 25490395): "dermal fibroblasts do indeed impact the degree of skin pigmentation." That model showed more pigment, not less, with photo-aged fibroblasts: "A stimulation of pigmentation in the presence of the natural photo-aged fibroblasts was revealed".
- Oxidative stress. Zeeshan 2022, 30 patients, measured in blood (PMID 33894101): "the oxidative stress is increased in patients with IGH." Blood markers say nothing direct about the spot itself.

### Genes and heredity

No gene has been shown to cause IGH. What exists is weak and indirect.

- Families. Falabella 1987 (PMID 3805391): "A family aggregation survey disclosed a higher prevalence of idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis in the family of patients with idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis than in the control group." Shared families also share sun habits, so this does not separate genes from environment.
- Immune-system gene type. Arrunategui 2002, 47 people, all renal transplant patients or controls (PMID 12452995): "The presence of HLA-DQ3 in patients with IGH suggests a genetic basis in a group of renal transplant subjects." The group is small and unusual: [transplant patients usually take immune-suppressing drugs; the abstract does not say].
- One family sequenced. Wu 2018 (PMID 30030622) found a gene change behind a different disease in a family that also had IGH, and stated: "However, we found no relationship between this mutation and IGH."
- Look-alike inherited white spots exist but are different diseases: guttate white spots in a genetic blistering disease with EXPH5 and ENPP1 variants (PMID 35960249, "a deleterious-predicted variant in ENPP1, a gene associated with disturbed transfer of melanosomes to keratinocytes"), and congenital spots in identical twins (PMID 16334615). Neither is IGH.
- The "somatic mutation" idea. Wilson 1982 (PMID 6183861) wrote: "The evidence indicates an age-related somatic mutation of melanocytes." That paper measured cell numbers and activity, not DNA. It is a hypothesis from 1982; this scan found no study that sequenced melanocytes from an IGH spot to test it.
- Gene activity. No study comparing gene expression (RNA) between an IGH spot and the same person's normal skin was found in PubMed, and earlier searches found no IGH dataset in GEO, BioImage Archive, Zenodo or Figshare (`data/search_log.csv`, 2026-11-27). The ATG7 finding above (PMID 41453137) is one gene checked in IGH skin after it was found in cultured cells.

### Somatic mutations in sun-exposed skin (not IGH-specific)

"Somatic" mutations are DNA changes a cell picks up during life, mostly from UV here, as opposed to inherited ones. None of these studies looked at IGH spots. They describe the skin IGH grows in.

- Every cell differs. Tang 2020, single melanocytes sequenced (PMID 33029006): "Every cell in the human body has a unique set of somatic mutations". Also: "melanocytes from healthy skin commonly contained pathogenic mutations, although these mutations tended to be weakly oncogenic".
- The burden is high in normal-looking skin. Martincorena 2015, 234 eyelid biopsies from four people (PMID 25999502): "the burden of somatic mutations averaged two to six mutations per megabase per cell, similar to that seen in many cancers". And: "aged sun-exposed skin is a patchwork of thousands of evolving clones with over a quarter of cells carrying cancer-causing mutations while maintaining the physiological functions of epidermis."
- It rises with age and sun. Hernando 2021, 123 donors (PMID 33307203): "Our results reveal an exponential accumulation of UV-related somatic mutations with age". Kim 2023, 39 people (PMID 36716918): "Exposed skin harbored a median of 530 mutations (10.4/mb, range = 51-2,947), whereas nonexposed skin majorly exhibited significantly fewer mutations (median = 13, 0.25/mb, range = 1-166)."
- Limbs are included. Fowler 2021 (PMID 33087317): "The prevalence of NOTCH1 and FAT1 mutations in forearm, trunk, and leg skin was similar to that in keratinocyte cancers."
- UV treatment adds mutations. Fowler 2025, skin biopsied before and after a narrowband UVB course in 16 psoriasis patients (PMID 40317189): "The NB-UVB treatment course increased the number of mutations in skin." **Inference:** this matters because UV light is also used to treat white spots.
- Low-mutation melanocytes survive. Tandukar 2026, 297 melanocyte clones from 31 donors (PMID 42045571): "a population of melanocytes with low mutation burden persists in sun-exposed epidermis. These cells are smaller, less dendritic, and exhibit stem-like expression profiles". The authors propose, as a hypothesis, "that melanocytes in the hair follicle occupy a privileged niche, protected from ultraviolet radiation, but replenish the epidermis following photodamage."

### Stem cell reservoirs that could refill a spot

Two kinds of evidence bear on whether a spot can be refilled: what IGH spots do when disturbed, and where spare pigment cells live.

- IGH skin can repigment away from the body. Gilhar 1989 grafted IGH skin onto mice without an immune system (PMID 2508572): "Twenty days after skin transplantation, repigmentation of the area was observed. The number of melanocytes increased significantly (388.75 +/- 213 mm2)." The authors concluded "that systemic factors may play a role in the pathogenesis of vitiligo and acquired hypomelanosis guttata." Patient numbers are `not in abstract`.
- Injury can bring cells back, partly. Ploysangam 1990 (PMID 2229495): "The number of dopa-positive melanocytes was significantly greater in the repigmented areas than in untreated lesions but less than in normal skin."
- Moving normal skin in did not help. Falabella 1987 (PMID 3805391): "Minigrafts of normal skin implanted in idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis lesions did not modify the achromic defects".
- Hair follicles hold a reserve (mostly mouse and vitiligo work, not IGH). Chou 2013, mouse (PMID 23749232): "after wounding or ultraviolet type B irradiation, melanocyte stem cells (McSCs) in the hair follicle exit the stem cell niche before their initial cell division, potentially depleting the pool of these cells." In human vitiligo treated with narrowband UVB, Goldstein 2015 (PMID 25822579): "NBUVB was associated with a significant increase in the number of melanocytes in the infundibulum and with restoration of the normal melanocyte population in the epidermis".
- The reserve wears out with age (hair, mouse). Nishimura 2005 (PMID 15618488): "hair graying is caused by defective self-maintenance of melanocyte stem cells." Sun 2023, mouse (PMID 37076619): "During ageing, there is accumulation of stranded McSCs that do not contribute to the regeneration of melanocyte progeny."
- Melanocyte numbers in sun-exposed skin overall. Gilchrest 1979, paired biopsies from 8 donors (PMID 88488): "Density of dopa-positive melanocytes was roughly twofold higher in the exposed than in the nonexposed skin at all ages" and "Melanocyte density declined approximately 6 to 8% of the surviving population per decade in both sites."
- Melanocytes can be grown in the lab from reprogrammed skin fibroblasts (iPS cells). Ohta 2011 (PMID 21249204): "Seven weeks after inducing differentiation, pigmented cells expressing melanocyte markers such as MITF, tyrosinase, SILV, and TYRP1, were detected." That was a lab method, not a treatment.

### What is not established

Fairly consistent across sources (small studies, mostly abstracts): an IGH spot has fewer melanocytes and less melanin than the skin next to it, and the remaining melanocytes look less active.

Reported by one or two small groups, not yet confirmed: poor pigment hand-over to keratinocytes; senescence markers (p16, p21, ATG7 loss) in the spot; scarring or senescent fibroblasts in the dermis below; raised oxidative stress in blood.

Not established at all:

- **A cause.** Every IGH study found is a snapshot. None changed a suspected cause and watched spots appear or clear.
- **A gene.** No gene, inherited or acquired, has been linked to IGH by sequencing. The somatic mutation idea (PMID 6183861) has not been tested in IGH cells.
- **Which cell is at fault first.** The melanocyte, the keratinocyte that receives pigment, and the fibroblast below all have some support. The papers do not show which change comes first.
- **Same-person comparisons for the molecular work.** The p16, p21 and hp1 comparison (PMID 29516488) is against healthy controls, not the same person's normal skin.
- **Anything at six months.** No source here follows a spot's cells over time.

### What this means for a "genetic fix" (inference)

Everything in this section is **inference** from the sources above, written because Ben asked to be told where the logic may be off. It is not a finding.

1. **The idea that genetic damage causes the spots is a hypothesis, not a finding.** Sun-exposed skin is full of mutations (PMID 25999502, PMID 33029006), and IGH is common in older, sun-exposed skin. But both facts are also true of skin with no white spots. No study has compared the DNA of melanocytes inside a spot with those just outside it. That comparison is the missing first step.
2. **Gene therapy usually fixes one known gene; sun damage is not one gene.** Gene therapies that work today replace or correct a single gene that is broken the same way in every cell. The sources above describe the opposite: "Every cell in the human body has a unique set of somatic mutations" (PMID 33029006), spread across "thousands of evolving clones" (PMID 25999502). There is no single target to correct, and editing millions of cells each at different sites is not something current tools do. [Whether any gene therapy is approved for skin, and for what, is a lead to check with a logged search; this page does not cite one.]
3. **The best-supported damage is missing or inactive cells, so the closest complete fix is replacing cells, not editing them.** Spots have fewer melanocytes (PMID 9765023), IGH skin can repigment when moved (PMID 2508572), and low-mutation, stem-like melanocytes persist in sun-exposed skin (PMID 42045571). Together these point toward supplying or waking up healthy pigment cells. That direction already has old IGH precedents (see the frontier scan) but no modern six-month study.
4. **The neighbourhood may matter as much as the cell.** If the dermis under a spot is scarred (PMID 35114009) or full of senescent fibroblasts (PMID 31823346, full text needed), new melanocytes might fade again. That is exactly what the project's six-month test would catch.
5. **Senescence markers could be a result, not a cause.** A cell that has lost its pigment job could become senescent afterwards. Telling these apart needs a time series or an experiment, not one biopsy.
6. **On garage gene therapy:** melanocytes are the cells melanoma starts from, and normal sun-exposed skin already carries cancer-gene mutations in "over a quarter of cells" (PMID 25999502). Pushing these cells to divide or editing their DNA is not low-risk. This is work for a lab with ethics approval, which is the route the study proposal already takes.

### Open questions

Each of these is a question a professional study could answer. None is answered by the sources above.

1. Do melanocytes inside an IGH spot carry more DNA damage (mutations or UV signatures) than melanocytes 5 mm away in the same person? (Method exists: single-melanocyte sequencing, PMID 33029006.)
2. Which genes are switched on or off in a spot compared with the same person's normal skin? No such dataset was found.
3. Does the damage start in the melanocyte, the keratinocyte that receives pigment, or the dermis below?
4. Are senescence markers in spots a cause or a result? Would a spot change if senescent cells were cleared? (Senolytics are discussed in the frontier scan as leads only.)
5. Are there still follicle reserve cells under an IGH spot, and can they be woken up?
6. If healthy melanocytes are put into a spot, do they survive and match colour six months after the final treatment?
7. Paywalled full texts that would sharpen this page: arbache2022 (PMID 35114009), rani2018 (PMID 29516488), kim2020 (PMID 31823346), kim2023 (PMID 36394380), kim2026 (PMID 41453137). Added to the paywall list in `STATUS.md`.
