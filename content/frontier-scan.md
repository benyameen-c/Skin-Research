## Frontier scan: experimental treatments for established IGH spots

### What this page is

<p><span class="tag human">Leads only</span> Nothing on this page is in the evidence ledger, and nothing here was extracted or verified. It is research, not medical advice.</p>

Ben asked whether newer, experimental kinds of treatment could help IGH spots that are already established (not prevention or early onset). On 27 November 2026 a logged search set looked for that: 21 PubMed queries, 14 ClinicalTrials.gov queries and 3 GEO queries (`data/search_log.csv`, lines marked "frontier scan"). Titles were screened. Abstracts were read only for the records cited here.

How to read it:

- Every claim names a saved source (PMID, NCT number or GEO accession). Numbers are quoted word for word from that source.
- Most sources are about **vitiligo** or other conditions, not IGH. Each item says which. Vitiligo is an immune attack on melanocytes; IGH is not known to be. A result in vitiligo is a reason to look, not evidence for IGH.
- Abstracts only. No full text was read for this page.
- The six-month test from `CLAUDE.md` (colour match lasting six months after the final treatment) is noted where a source says anything about it.

### What is known about why an IGH spot stays white

Any experimental treatment has to target a cause, so this comes first. All items in this section are **IGH** studies, and all are small.

- **Melanocytes are fewer, and the remaining ones look senescent.** Six IGH patients versus six controls: "expression of p16, p21, and hp1 was significantly higher in lesional skin of IGH patient as compared to healthy controls", and IGH melanocytes were "bigger in size with very small and retracted dendrites" (PMID 29516488). A 2023 letter is titled "Melanocytes in idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis disappear and are senescent" (PMID 36394380; title only, no abstract saved).
- **The surrounding dermis may be part of it.** A 2020 paper is titled "Role of senescent fibroblasts in the development of idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis" (PMID 31823346; title only, no abstract saved).
- **An early molecular change has been named.** In UVB-induced senescent melanocytes, "ATG7 downregulation emerged as the earliest molecular alteration and was consistently found in senescent melanocytes and idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis skin" (PMID 41453137). Metformin "restored autophagic activity" in cells. The authors frame it as prevention ("to prevent melanocyte ageing"), and no patient was treated.
- **IGH skin can repigment when moved off the patient.** IGH skin grafted onto nude mice: melanocytes in the spot were "55.25 +/- 18.00 mm2 vs 220 +/- 28.28 mm2" in surrounding skin; "Twenty days after skin transplantation, repigmentation of the area was observed" (PMID 2508572, 1989). The authors read this as "systemic factors may play a role". [Inference: whatever holds the spot white may not be fixed in the tissue itself.]
- **Adding melanocytes works at least briefly.** Blister-roof grafts in IGH and other leukodermas: "Repigmentation was visible within 7 to 14 days" (PMID 3908515, 1985). No later follow-up in the abstract. This paper is already in the ledger as suvanprakorn1985.

[Inference: two kinds of approach follow from this. Replace the missing melanocytes (Direction 4), or wake or protect the ones left (Directions 5 and 6). Directions 1 to 3 come mostly from vitiligo, where the problem is immune attack, so they fit IGH less directly.]

### Direction 1: gene and RNA medicines

**Status: no human study in any white-spot condition found.** Three PubMed queries (IGH plus gene terms, vitiligo plus gene terms, topical nucleic acids in pigment cells) and two ClinicalTrials.gov queries. The registry search for gene therapy in hypopigmentation found "0 gene therapy trials for acquired hypopigmentation" (search log). The only IGH hits were the senescence papers above.

What exists, all outside IGH:

- **siRNA against immune signalling, in mice and skin explants (vitiligo model, not IGH).** A JAK1-selective siRNA: "A single injection into mouse skin enables five weeks of duration of effect" (PMID 37925520). A related siRNA against the interferon-gamma receptor reports "effective IFNGR1 protein reduction for at least 1 month in mice" (PMID 35477658). Both target immune signalling, which is a vitiligo mechanism, not a known IGH one.
- **siRNA has been applied topically to human pigment problems, but to remove pigment, not add it.** In melasma, an MITF siRNA cream gave "a 90.4% lightening of the siRNA-treated lesions toward normal skin color" after 12 weeks (PMID 21119619). Spherical nucleic acids were used the same way, to lighten (PMID 33433203). [Inference: these show a topical nucleic acid can reach human pigment cells, which is the hard part; the direction of effect would have to be reversed.]
- **Pushing pigment up by gene transfer exists only in cells.** A human tyrosinase plasmid in niosomes raised melanin production in mouse melanoma cells, "13.03-, 8.46-, 5.36-, and 6.58-folds of control" (PMID 22958397). A TYRP-1 siRNA study reports knockdown "(> 80%)" in cultured cells (PMID 29560584).
- **Delivery vectors are being built for skin.** An AAV capsid review-style report: expression "peaked at 1 month and persisted for up to 2 years" in mice, with "preferential targeting of dermal fibroblasts and hair follicle bulge cells" (PMID 42070083). It names vitiligo as an application. No white-spot condition was treated.

[Inference: for IGH this is a long way off. There is no target gene established in IGH beyond the ATG7 observation (PMID 41453137), and that paper's own framing is prevention.]
### Direction 2: JAK inhibitors

**Status: approved in vitiligo, one IGH hit and it is not a JAK study.** The IGH-plus-JAK query returned a single record, PMID 34084020, which is the excimer light and bimatoprost case already in the ledger as kreeshan2021. No JAK trial in IGH was found in PubMed or the registry.

Why it still matters here: this is the only white-spot condition where a drug has been taken through phase 3 with durability data, so it sets the bar for what a trial in IGH would have to show.

- **Phase 3 in vitiligo.** Ruxolitinib cream 1.5% twice daily: at week 24 the share reaching a 75% improvement in facial score was "29.8% in the ruxolitinib-cream group and 7.4% in the vehicle group" in one trial and "30.9% and 11.4%, respectively" in the other (PMID 36260792). Face only, and these are improvement scores, not colour match.
- **Durability, measured from withdrawal.** In the extension study NCT04530344, patients who had reached a 90% facial improvement were re-randomised to keep the cream or switch to vehicle. Time to loss of that 90% response had a median of "195.0" days (95% CI "113.0" to "372.0") on vehicle, and was not estimable on continued cream "because there were too few events" (registry results, 56 and 55 patients). Time to relapse below 75% was not estimable in either arm.
- [Inference: this is the clearest durability dataset in any white-spot condition, and it says the effect is maintained by continuing the drug. A six-month match after a *final* treatment is a different question, and this trial does not answer it: the arm that stopped lost its response at a median of about 6.4 months.]

[Inference: a JAK inhibitor is unlikely to suit IGH on mechanism. It calms an immune attack, and no immune attack has been established in IGH. The IGH senescence findings point elsewhere.]

### Direction 3: melanocortin and prostaglandin drugs

These push pigment production rather than fixing a cause, so they are candidates for a spot that still has some melanocytes.

**Afamelanotide** (an alpha-MSH analogue implant). No IGH study found; 25 vitiligo records and 5 registry trials. In vitiligo with NB-UVB, repigmentation was "48.64% (95% CI, 39.49%-57.80%) at day 168" with the combination versus "33.26% (95% CI, 24.18%-42.33%)" with phototherapy alone (PMID 25230094, 28 versus 27 patients). It is a systemic implant for a cosmetic complaint of a few small spots. [Inference: the risk-benefit looks wrong for IGH, and nothing suggests the colour holds after the implants stop.]

**Prostaglandin analogues** (latanoprost, bimatoprost), applied topically. No IGH trial found. The one IGH record in this query set, kreeshan2021 (PMID 34084020), is a single patient treated with excimer light plus topical bimatoprost and is already in the ledger. In vitiligo the results are mixed: a bimatoprost trial on non-facial vitiligo failed its own endpoint, "Because of a lack of response observed for the primary end point, a post hoc analysis with a less stringent definition of response" (PMID 27272076, 11/10/11 patients). An eyelid trial reported the drug group "showed significant reduction in the symptoms of the disease" (PMID 31758835, 31 patients).

One registry trial is close to Ben's question in design, though not in condition: NCT06122090, laser-assisted bimatoprost on hypopigmented scars, 25 patients estimated, randomised and triple-masked, primary outcome "area of pigmentation within control and treated scars" at "follow-up number 6, an average of 8 months". Status "UNKNOWN". [Inference: scars are not IGH, but a within-person control plus an 8-month endpoint is the shape a useful IGH trial would take.]

### Direction 4: cell transplantation

**This is the one direction with long follow-up and a colour-match endpoint, and the one with a direct IGH precedent.** The cells are taken from the patient's own normal skin, separated into a suspension, and put onto the prepared white area. Most of the literature is vitiligo.

IGH precedent, both old and both already in the project's records:

- Blister-roof grafting in IGH: "Repigmentation was visible within 7 to 14 days" (PMID 3908515, ledger id suvanprakorn1985). Nothing later in the abstract.
- The nude-mouse graft study above (PMID 2508572) shows IGH skin repigmenting once grafted, which is the biological argument for trying it.

Durability in vitiligo and other leukodermas (none of these is IGH):

- 23 patients, 24 lesions, Thailand: at 12 months "an 80.8% ± 19.3% VASI improvement, which was sustained over 84 months with an 80%-90% VASI improvement" (PMID 39507768).
- 2,283 patients, follow-up "12-108months": "Excellent repigmentation was achieved in 400/606 (66%)" for segmental vitiligo (PMID 33582653).
- 602 patients followed to six years, where relapse was defined as "re-appearance of more than 10% depigmentation in a previously treated and repigmented site"; non-segmental vitiligo raised relapse risk ("adjusted HR = 2.11; P = 0.03") (PMID 30793805).
- 142 patients to six years: "Eighty (56%) patients showed excellent" repigmentation, "which was retained till the end of the respective follow-up period" (PMID 16207186).

A registered trial uses almost exactly the project's endpoint, in non-vitiligo leukoderma: NCT07662915, 22 patients estimated, cell suspension versus the Jodhpur technique plus excimer laser, primary outcome "Mean Percentage Repigmentation of Target Lesions" at "6 months" and a secondary outcome "Color Match Assessment" at "6 months". Status "NOT_YET_RECRUITING", and its eligibility does not name IGH (it requires "Clinically diagnosed stable non-vitiligo leukoderma" and excludes vitiligo).

[Inference: this is the most promising direction on the evidence, but it is a surgical procedure, and the question for IGH is proportionality: a 2 to 5 mm spot on a forearm against a donor site, dermabrasion and cost. The older alternative, grafting one spot at a time, is in the ledger and only ever reported short follow-up. Nobody appears to have tested a cell suspension on IGH spots with a six-month colour-match readout. That is a gap, not a conclusion.]

### Direction 5: Wnt and melanocyte stem cells

The idea is to call up the pigment stem cells that sit in nearby hair follicles instead of transplanting cells. **Status: laboratory only. "0 Wnt agonist trials" in the registry (search log), and no IGH study.**

- "Wnt3a induces hair follicle regeneration and McSC activation", and so does Wnt10b, in cells and mice (PMID 27138625).
- A controlled micro-injury device in model mice "induces the hair follicle melanocyte stem cells to migrate upwards to the interfollicular epidermis", with the authors attributing it to "the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway" (PMID 35044224). [Inference: this is the mechanism usually offered for why microneedling and laser work at all, which makes Direction 7 the cheap end of this same idea.]
- Fluoxetine activated the same stem cells in mice and zebrafish through a serotonin receptor and Wnt (PMID 42716209). Animals only.
- In vitiligo patients, a GEO dataset backs the pathway: GSE65127, "Targeting the WNT pathway for repigmenting vitiligo", 40 samples.

[Inference: relevant to IGH in principle, because IGH spots sit on hairy sun-exposed forearms and shins where follicles are present, and because the problem may be melanocytes that stopped working rather than an immune attack. But there is no drug to try, so for Ben this is a reading direction, not a testable one.]

### Direction 6: senolytics and senescence

**This direction matches what the IGH mechanism papers actually report, and it is the least developed.** The registry search found "no registered senolytic trial in vitiligo" (search log), and none in IGH.

The chain of reasoning in the sources:

1. IGH lesional skin shows raised senescence markers p16, p21 and hp1 (PMID 29516488), a letter says IGH melanocytes "disappear and are senescent" (PMID 36394380, title only) and another implicates senescent fibroblasts (PMID 31823346, title only).
2. Senescent melanocytes are not passive: clearing them with a senolytic "suppressed" their damaging effect on neighbouring keratinocytes in a skin model (PMID 31633821).
3. Senolytics can kill senescent melanocytes in a dish: "only ABT-737 and ABT-263 showed a significant reduction in the number of SA-β-Gal-positive senescent melanocytes" (PMID 39846383). A separate letter proposes ABT263 as a "potential skin-lightening candidate" for photoageing pigmentation (PMID 34773647, title only).
4. In humans, senolytics have been given only for other diseases, for example an open-label pilot in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (PMID 30616998).

[Inference: there is a direction-of-effect problem that the sources make visible. These drugs are being discussed as skin *lightening* agents (PMID 34773647), because killing a senescent melanocyte removes it. In IGH the melanocytes are already fewer. Clearing senescent cells from the spot would only help if something then repopulates it, which no source here tests. The metformin paper (PMID 41453137) takes the opposite tack, preserving melanocytes rather than clearing them, and Ben has already ruled prevention out of scope.]

[Inference: the open question worth writing down is whether a senescent IGH spot can be repopulated at all once cleared, and that is a laboratory question, not something an engineer can test.]

### Direction 7: microneedling and PRP

The cheap, already-available end of Direction 5. **The IGH query returned zero records:** `"guttate hypomelanosis" AND ("platelet-rich plasma" OR PRP OR microneedl* OR exosome* OR "extracellular vesicle*" OR "growth factor*")` gave 0 hits (search log).

The nearest trial is in the adjacent diagnosis already in the ledger as elhoshy2025 (PMID 39584682, localised acquired hypomelanosis, 20 patients, lesions randomised to one microneedling session or no treatment). It is worth reading again in this context because its control arm did as well as its treated arm: "Median peripheral tanning was observed in 80% of lesions in the microneedling arm versus 85% in the no-treatment arm", and "Surface tanning showed a median increase of +3 grades in both arms". The authors still conclude microneedling "deserves a place in the armamentarium".

[Inference: that result is a warning about this whole field rather than about microneedling alone. An untreated white spot on a sun-exposed limb can look better at follow-up, so any single-arm or uncontrolled IGH result is hard to trust. It supports the project's existing insistence on a within-person untreated control.]

Vitiligo work in this direction is large but combination-heavy: PRP added to lasers or phototherapy (PMIDs 40386849, 38878236, 30510437), and microneedling added to tacrolimus or 5-FU (PMIDs 39992062, 36718813). The registry lists 5 vitiligo PRP trials "all status unknown" and 2 exosome trials, "both not yet recruiting" (search log). [Inference: combinations make it impossible to tell what did the work, and none of this is IGH.]

One mechanistic thread connects to a treatment already in the ledger: 5-FU, used in the arbache tattoo-infusion studies, has a pro-pigment effect in mice through fibroblast signalling, "CXCL12 immunostaining was significantly increased in the dermal compartment of wounds treated with 5-FU (control vs. 5-FU, 22.47 ± 8.85 vs. 44.69 ± 5.97, P < 0.05)" (PMID 34507320). Mouse wounds, not IGH.

### Where an engineer without a clinic could contribute (inference)

<p><span class="tag sim">Inference</span> Everything in this section is my reasoning from the sources above, not a finding from any of them. Ben asked specifically what he could work on. He is a manufacturing engineer, with no clinic, no patients and no laboratory.</p>

The honest summary of the directions above: the biology is not an engineering problem, and none of it can be moved from a workshop. What repeatedly *is* broken, in paper after paper, is the measurement.

1. **Measurement is the bottleneck, and it is a measurement problem.** The project has already found that no IGH study reports a colour match holding six months after the final treatment, and that the longest interval anyone states is two months. The sources on this page show why the field cannot easily do better: outcomes are investigator grades and improvement percentages, and in the nearest controlled trial the untreated control arm scored as well as the treated arm (PMID 39584682). A spot that is measured by eye against a remembered baseline cannot support a six-month claim. Building a repeatable way to measure spot-versus-surrounding colour is work Ben can do alone, and it is already the subject of the project's measurement-tool plan.
2. **Specifying the trial is also available work.** NCT06122090 and NCT07662915 show the design that answers the question: within-person control, a stated endpoint at six to eight months, a colour-match outcome. Neither is in IGH. Writing that protocol precisely for IGH, with the endpoint defined in measurable terms, is a document, not a clinic. The project's study proposal is this.
3. **Re-analysis of what is already public is available work.** The GEO sets above are downloadable. [Inference, flagged as weak: with three samples a side, re-analysis would produce a hypothesis at best, and an engineer with no bioinformatics background would be spending a long time for a weak result. I would not start here.]
4. **The durability question can be attacked without any new treatment.** Nothing on this page needs a new drug to establish how long an existing, already-used IGH treatment holds. A repeatable measurement plus a stated interval from the final session would be a contribution to the field on its own, and it is the gap the ledger has documented.

What is not available to him, stated plainly so it is not re-opened later: anything involving senolytics, siRNA, AAV vectors, cell suspensions or drugs on skin. Those need a laboratory or a clinic and ethical approval, and self-experiment is not research.

[Inference: if Ben wants the shortest path from here to something presentable in the field, it is the measurement tool plus the protocol, with this scan as the background section that says why the experimental directions were set aside.]

### Gaps and what was not run

- **Two senolytic queries did not run.** `tools/pubmed.py` "stopped with HTTP 400 (efetch) after esearch, twice"; the lines are logged `NOT RUN` in `data/search_log.csv` and were replaced by two simpler queries (3 and 44 hits). The senolytic reading above rests on those replacements.
- **One registry search was not screened past its first page.** `query.cond=leukoderma` returned 449 trials, "not screened beyond the first page" (search log). Any IGH-adjacent cell-transplant trial could be sitting in the remainder.
- **Titles only for most records.** 383 records were saved; abstracts were read for about 40. The rest were screened on title. A relevant paper could have been passed over.
- **Three IGH mechanism papers have no abstract on PubMed**: PMIDs 36394380, 31823346, and 34773647 for the senolytic claim. Those three claims rest on titles alone and are marked as such above. If Ben can supply the full texts, they are the most load-bearing missing sources on this page.
- **No full text was read for this page at all**, and nothing here passed an extraction or verification gate.
- **Web of Science, Scopus and Embase were not searched** (no access), so this is a PubMed-and-registry view.
